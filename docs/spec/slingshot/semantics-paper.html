What Slingshot did, and what a scaling policy should mean

Slingshot evaluates a scaling policy with a handful of behaviors that a reader of the model does not see. The decisions below keep two of those behaviors, replace the rest with the behavior the model states, and replace the group-size rule with a third one: an out-of-range step is clamped to the bound, not discarded.

Compiled from semantics-decisions.json, saved 4 November 2026. Figures that cite a run use seed 1 and --flavour slingshot on the five corpus-spd examples. Where a decision is not what --spd-quirks fixed does today, the figure says so.

1. How to read a decision

Each quirk is a place where the running interpreter and the text of the model can disagree. Three answers were available, and the notes use all three.

  • Slingshot The interpreter’s behavior is the right semantics. Reproducing it is not a compatibility mode. It is what the mechanism means.
  • Fixed The model’s stated behavior is the right semantics. The interpreter was doing something else, and that something else should stop.
  • Other Neither reading. The note states the rule.

One decision is still empty (Q1). Two are marked not sure (Q12, Q26). Q16 is marked fixed, with a reservation in the note. The map is the whole set.

2. The system the figures use

Five of the examples are the same small system, with a different policy file. An open workload calls chargeForEspresso every 3 seconds. A load balancer forwards the call to EspressoAccounting, deployed on AppServer. The elastic infrastructure’s unit is that container: a scale-out copies it. A service-group policy, where there is one, scales the accounting assembly, and each new replica is placed on its own container.

3. A time policy hears only its own clock

Slingshot posts SimulationTimeReached to every policy of the target group (Q15). A policy written to fire at its own instant also fires at every other policy’s instant. In Same instant two policies share t = 50 s: step +2, and step −1. Each reacts to the other’s event, so the pair runs twice.

The adopted semantics gives a policy only the event of its own trigger. The pair runs once. The group ends at 2 servers, which is what “+2 and −1 at the same instant” reads as, starting from 1. Ending at 3 is an extra, invisible firing.

What it meant: a model with several time policies on one group does not have the schedule it writes down. Adding a scale-in “at 80” also wakes the scale-out “at 50”, and the other way around. What the change does: the schedule on the page is the schedule that runs. Policies still share a group, a cooldown, and a size bound. They do not share a clock.

4. Group size clamps the step. It does not drop the decision.

Slingshot’s filter chain holds the thrashing constraint and not the group-size constraint (Q9). minSize and maxSize are applied later, inside the QVTo transformation, by rewriting the number of containers. SimOxide’s fixed flag does something stronger than that: if the step would be clamped to zero, the filter disregards the policy before the adjustment, and no cooldown is started.

The decision rejects both. The note says the out-of-range decision must be modified to the bound, not ignored, and that this modification belongs to the transformation, where Slingshot already put it.

it has to make sure if the change is lt or gt, then the decision is not simply ignored while it lays out of the bounds, but the decision has to be modified to max and min. This was part of the qvto transformation and not in the filter chain in Slingshot.

What it meant under Slingshot: the numbers minSize and maxSize on the group are real, but only as a rewrite of the adjustment, after the policy has already been accepted. What the fixed flag meant: those numbers can veto the policy. What the adopted rule means: the bound is a rewrite of the decision. A scale-out above the maximum still scales, up to the maximum. A scale-in below the minimum still scales, down to the minimum. A decision that lands exactly on the bound is a modified decision of size zero. It is not a disregard.

5. Cooldown counts the attempt, including an attempt that does not change capacity

This is the decision that keeps Slingshot. The adjustor records the time, the cooldown, and the group’s last policy when the request is made (Q2), including when the following transformation leaves the number of containers unchanged. The note states why that is the semantics rather than a bug.

a cooldown is a constraint on an adjustment made to the target, even though that adjustment may not effectively change the capacity of the target group

Read next to the size rule, the two decisions are one mechanism. The policy fires. The transformation clamps the step onto [minSize, maxSize]. The cooldown starts because an adjustment was made to the target, whether or not the clamp left the capacity where it was. The fixed flag breaks that pair: it drops the zero step first, so the cooldown never starts, and a second policy at the same instant is free to scale the other way.

What it meant: cooldown in Slingshot is not “time since the capacity last changed”. It is “time since an adjustment was last offered to this target”. A policy author who sets 180 seconds is limiting how often the group may be the subject of a decision. What the change relative to the fixed flag does: it stops treating a clamped no-op as if the policy had not run. What the change relative to a careless reading of Slingshot does: the clamp is explicit. The group does not grow past its maximum, and the attempt is still on the record.

6. A removed container finishes the work it already has

The specification of Q5 says Slingshot aborts one job on the removed container and loses the others. The decision chooses drain: work already there finishes, new requests go elsewhere. The note adds a reading of what Palladio is actually doing.

I think also in Palladio/Slingshot the container drains, the resource container is deleted but the queue in the underlying data structure is cleaned up only after

So the adopted semantics is drain, and the recorded suspicion is that the apparent loss is the order of cleanup — the container object goes first, the queue behind it is emptied later — rather than a rule that jobs should vanish. The paper does not treat that suspicion as a new measurement. It records it next to the decision.

7. A new container joins the links of its unit

After an adjustment, Slingshot reconnects every linking resource to every container (Q6). The adopted rule is local: a new container is appended to the links its unit is already on; other links stay; a removed container leaves every link. In the running example there is only one link, so both rules draw the same picture. The difference is the second link the example does not have.

What it meant: a scale-out rewrote the network, not only the copied server. Latency and routing after an adaptation depended on links the policy never named. What the change does: the network of the unit is the network of the replica. Anything else stays the model’s.

8. Top-down scale-in removes the emptied container in the same enactment

Slingshot’s top-down transformation deletes a container that the scale-in has emptied only at the next enactment (Q7). The decision takes the same-enactment removal, which is the transformation’s own TODO, and then separates two ideas the late deletion had been carrying.

The fix is the right top-down transformation semantic. It was not deleted to account for requests in transit. A top-down scale-in assumes that the effect of scaling in components automatically translates to the scaling in of hosts. That does not need to be the case. In practice, these two can be controlled independently.

What the late delete meant: the transformation was remembering work in flight by leaving a dead container in the model for one more decision. What the change does: in-flight work is the drain rule (section 6), and the structure of the model updates when the decision does. A designer who wants the host to stay says so with an infrastructure policy, not by relying on a one-step lag.

9. Windows, order, and the things that must be determined by the model

One window per element

Slingshot pours every element of the group into a single aggregation window, reads it on measurements that are not the stimulus, and consumes the EMA’s emit slot even when a later filter disregards the policy (Q13). The adopted rule is one window per measuring point, aggregated across elements only for the stimulus’s metric, and consumed only when the policy fires. The five time-triggered examples never open a window. The rule matters as soon as a group has two replicas and a CPU or response-time trigger, which is the RMUC HPA case after the first scale-out: a merged window lets an idle new replica dilute the busy original, or the other way around, inside one average.

Relative step of zero

If a relative percentage comes out as no container, Slingshot discards minAdjustmentValue. The adopted rule (Q4) leaves a true zero as zero, and applies the minimum only when the percentage does call for a change, in that direction. A 0 % policy does not secretly move by its minimum. A small non-zero percentage is not rounded away.

Placement and container order

Slingshot picks a container for a new assembly with any(true), so the choice follows Java identity-hash order and can differ between JVMs (Q8). After the adjustment, the list of containers is in that same hash order (Q16). Both decisions take insertion order, which is what the deterministic runner already uses. The note on Q8 asks what the harm is. The note on Q16 says the choice is not settled.

The harm is the next decision, not the hash itself. Scale-in removes the newest element. If two machines disagree on which container was created last, they remove different servers, and the response-time series diverge with no change in the model. Insertion order makes “newest” a property of the run, not of the JVM. Q16 stays on the open list because the note does.

The model after a removal, and a run that cannot share a JVM

Slingshot leaves removed assemblies, monitors, and passive resources in the running model, and a population change does not take effect (Q14). The adopted rule reports the deletions and drops those monitors. Static fields in TargetGroupUtils (Q10) are not reproduced: two runs in one process must not see each other’s group size.

An unsupported trigger is an error, not a silent empty SPD

Slingshot throws during set-up and then drops the whole policy definition, so the run continues with no scaling at all (Q11). The adopted rule fails the configuration and names the stimulus. A model that asks for a metric the interpreter does not have should not look like a model that chose not to scale.

The aggregator stays

AVERAGE is the exponential moving average (60 s, emit every 10 s, α = 0.2). MAX, MIN, MEDIAN, and SUM keep the last ten values (Q3). This one is kept. The decay is the correction for samples that exist only when a job arrives or leaves. It is the design of the trigger, not an accident next to it.

10. Composed triggers are not decided

Slingshot’s AND continues after a child has failed, OR does not evaluate its children, and XOR inverts after the first child (Q12). The fixed proposal evaluates every child on every event and combines them as AND, OR, or exactly-one. The decision is not sure, and the note asks the question the proposal skips.

on every event or on relevant events?

That question is still the semantics. “Every event” means a time event of one child re-evaluates the other children, which is a cousin of Q15. “Relevant events” means each child is evaluated on the measurements or the instant that belong to it, and the combination is of those results. The five examples have no composed trigger, so they do not answer it. Until that choice is made, Q12 has no adopted rule.

11. The engine

Q17 to Q37 are not scaling policies. They are places where a request, a resource, or a monitor does something other than the Palladio action it is written as. Thirty of them take the fixed reading: demands of one internal action run in order; a loop of count 0 does not enter; roles resolve along the connector that names them; forks get their own cursors; delay resources wait; response-time monitors record; composites are entered; a call with nowhere to go stops the run instead of disposing the link handler for every later transfer. The full list is the table at the end.

Two engine decisions are not in that majority.

Q24 Acquire and Release keep Slingshot’s requirement of a ParametricResourceDemand. The fixed reading, which acquires one unit when the demand is missing, is rejected. An acquire with no amount is a broken action, not an implicit acquire of one. The model has to say how much.

Q26 A superseded processor-sharing completion that still samples state and utilisation is not decided. The fixed reading would record nothing. The Slingshot reading records an extra sample. No note chooses between a measurement that includes stale events and one that does not.

Q1, whether a disregard restores adjustor state, was left unanswered. No example in this set separates the two, and no effect on results is known. It stays out of the adopted semantics until there is a case that can show it.

12. What is better, in one picture

Better here means: a result can be read off the policy file without knowing which events are shared, which constraints are enforced only later, and which containers an adjustment will wire together. The adopted semantics is that reading, with two deliberate exceptions and one rewrite.

  • The schedule, the links, the windows, the order, and the contents of the model after a removal are what the file says.
  • A size bound rewrites the step onto the bound. It does not veto the policy.
  • A cooldown starts when the adjustment is made to the target, including when the rewritten step leaves the capacity unchanged.
  • A removed container finishes work it has already accepted. In-flight work is not a reason to keep the container in the structure for another enactment.
  • Component cardinality and host cardinality are separate policies. Top-down removal is same-enactment only when the policy really is top-down.
  • An acquire names its demand. An unsupported trigger is an error.

The current --spd-quirks fixed flag is not this semantics. It matches the adopted rule on Q4, Q6, Q7, Q8, Q13, Q14, Q15, and Q16, and it disagrees on the two decisions that change the RMUC traces: it disregards a zero step (against Q9) and it withholds cooldown until the capacity changes (against Q2). A run that claims to follow this paper has to clamp in the transformation and commit the cooldown on the request. The engine flag for Q24 has to stay on the Slingshot side as well: a missing demand is an error.
